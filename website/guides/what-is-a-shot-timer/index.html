What is a shot timer and how does it work?

Updated 26 November 2026 · 4 min read

A shot timer is the stopwatch of practical shooting, with one difference: it starts you with a beep, and your own shots stop the clock. This page explains what it measures, how it hears a shot and what the numbers tell you.

The short answer

A shot timer, also called a shooting timer, gives a start beep and then listens. Its microphone hears each shot and stores when it was fired, counted from the beep. That gives you the time to your first shot, the split between each pair of shots and your total time. A shot timer is either a dedicated device you clip to your belt or an app on your phone.

How a shot timer works

  1. You press start. The timer waits for a start delay, which is fixed or random. A random delay keeps you from guessing the beep; in an IPSC match the start signal comes one to four seconds after “Stand by”.
  2. The beep starts the clock. It is loud and high-pitched so you hear it through ear protection.
  3. The microphone listens. Each shot gets a time, measured from the beep to a hundredth of a second.
  4. Your last shot is your time. Nobody has to press a button at the right moment, so the timer can time you when you train alone.

How it hears a shot

A gunshot is short and very loud, so a shot timer watches the volume: when the sound crosses a threshold, it counts a shot. The sensitivity setting moves that threshold. Set the sensitivity too high and the timer counts a neighbour's shots or a dropped magazine; set it too low and it misses yours.

Filters make the count more reliable. Simple Shot Timer ignores the echo right after a shot, keeps its own beep out of the count, and can be limited to the frequency band where your gun is loudest. How to use your phone as a shot timer shows how to set this up for your range.

What the numbers mean

Take a string of five shots that the timer heard at 1.42, 1.65, 1.91, 2.14 and 2.37 seconds.

  • First shot: 1.42. The time from the beep to your first shot. It includes your reaction to the beep and your draw or presentation.
  • Splits: 0.23, 0.26, 0.23, 0.23. The time between one shot and the next. On one target a split shows how fast you get the sights back; between two targets it shows your transition.
  • Total: 2.37. The time from the beep to your last shot.
  • Par time. A time limit you set yourself. The timer gives a second beep when it is up, and you try to finish before it.

The numbers show where the time went. A total of 2.37 with a first shot of 1.42 says that more than half of the string was the draw, so that's where practice pays off first.

Shot timer, shooting timer, par timer

“Shot timer” and “shooting timer” mean the same thing. “Shot timer” is the name you'll hear at matches, and app stores use both.

A par timer is something else: it gives the start beep and the par beep, but it doesn't listen. Most shot timers have a par mode, so a shot timer can do a par timer's job, but not the other way round. Par timer vs. shot timer explains when a par timer is enough.

Who uses a shot timer

Competition shooters, because time is part of the score. In IPSC and USPSA your points are divided by your time, and in IDPA and Steel Challenge the time itself is the score, with penalties added. Every stage is run on a shot timer, held next to the shooter by the range officer, or the safety officer in IDPA.

In training, a timer replaces a feeling with a number. “That felt fast” becomes 1.42 to the first shot, and next week you can check whether it is 1.35. Instructors, police and military trainers use shot timers for the same reason.

A device or an app

A dedicated shot timer is a small box with a loud buzzer, a microphone and a display, made for the range. It costs $100 or more. A shot timer app uses your phone's speaker and microphone instead, so the beep is quieter and the microphone wasn't made for gunshots, but you already own the hardware and many apps are free.

What a shot timer can't tell you

  • Whether you hit. It measures time only. A fast string of misses is still a fast string on the display, so score your targets as well.
  • Whose shot it was. It works by sound, so a shot from the next bay can count if it is as loud as yours.
  • Dry-fire clicks. The click of a dry-fire trigger press is far quieter than a shot. In dry fire you usually train against a par time instead.

Your first string

Start with one shot from the draw, or from a ready position if your range doesn't allow drawing. Set a random start delay, fire one shot at the beep and read the time. Do it five times and you know your first-shot time. That number is your baseline for everything else.

At home, the same drill works in dry fire against a par time. The dry-fire par drills guide has the safety rules and starting par times for five drills.
